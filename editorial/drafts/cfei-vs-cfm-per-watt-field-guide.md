# CFEI vs. CFM per Watt: How to Read an HVLS Fan Efficiency Claim

*HVLS 360° | Controls & Engineering*

Two large-diameter fans can move very different amounts of air while appearing surprisingly similar on an old-style efficiency comparison. That is part of why **Ceiling Fan Energy Index (CFEI)** matters.

For a facility manager buying a 20-foot-class fan—or an installer reviewing competing submittals—the useful question is not which catalog prints the biggest airflow number. It's whether the numbers were measured on a comparable basis, at the same operating point, and whether they describe the effect the project is actually paying for.

## What changed from CFM per watt

A cubic-feet-per-minute-per-watt (CFM/W) figure compares airflow with electrical input power at a stated operating point. The number can be useful, but it is not a complete picture of fan performance. Fan speed, blade geometry and the way results are aggregated can distort a simplistic efficiency ranking.

In a technical explanation published by AMCA International, a group of fans with the same older CFM/W rating had markedly different CFEI ratings at high speed [1]. That comparison is an illustration, not a real-world claim that any specific fan will save a given amount of electricity.

CFEI compares a fan's measured electrical input power against the power of a defined reference fan at a corresponding test condition [1][2]. In broad terms, a higher index represents lower energy use relative to that reference. The calculation uses the specialized methods referenced in federal rules; **it is not just another label for CFM/W**.

## Which fans are covered?

The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) defines a **large-diameter ceiling fan** by a blade span greater than **7 feet**, including wing tips where applicable [2]. The large-diameter regulatory category is broader than the industry's everyday use of the term HVLS: a large-diameter fan does not automatically qualify as an HVLS fan in every product or application sense.

AMCA explains that the CFEI method applies to covered large-diameter ceiling fans and that there are exceptions, including certain belt-driven products [2]. Don't assume that a floor fan, directional air circulator, exhaust fan or packaged air-handling unit should be evaluated under the identical rule. Those products can fall under other test procedures, metrics or regulatory categories [3].

## What to request from a manufacturer

A useful submittal should make it possible to compare the actual models you're considering—not just a marketing category.

1. **Exact model and blade span.** Include the airfoil or blade option, diameter, tip configuration and control package. A different configuration is not automatically the same rated product.
2. **Declared CFEI data and applicable test procedure.** Ask for the test basis and the speeds or duty points to which the figures apply. An unexplained efficiency number is not enough.
3. **Airflow, power and test context.** Look for the measured or calculated airflow and electrical input in watts, together with the laboratory method and conditions. An impressive airflow number alone does not tell you occupied-zone air speed.
4. **Independent rating evidence, where specified.** AMCA's Certified Ratings Program offers a way to look for products with certified published performance; having a certification logo is not the same as proving suitability for every installation [4].
5. **Project-specific installation and operating assumptions.** Mounting height, obstructions, racking, thermostat strategy and hours of operation belong in the performance discussion, even though they are not captured by one laboratory index.

For bidding, put those fields side by side. If a manufacturer cannot provide comparable data for the exact model, mark the comparison incomplete rather than filling the gaps with estimates.

## CFEI is not a cooling-performance guarantee

CFEI addresses energy performance against a reference. It does not tell you how quickly an occupied zone will feel comfortable, how evenly air moves through racking, or how much warm air a tall building will mix down in winter.

A comfort design needs a separate method for evaluating air speed, temperatures and occupancy. ASHRAE Standard 55-2023 addresses the interaction of environmental and personal thermal-comfort factors [5]. For ventilation and outside-air requirements, the relevant commercial framework is ASHRAE Standard 62.1, currently available in its 2025 edition [6]. The edition enforced on a particular project can differ by jurisdiction.

The commissioning plan therefore needs two sets of evidence: **what the fan is rated to do in a standardized test** and **what the installation delivers in the actual building**. They answer different questions.

## A note on changing rules

The DOE still lists federal ceiling-fan standards and test procedures on its official ceiling-fans page [7]. In an October 21, 2025 industry regulatory update, AMCA reported that an anticipated increase in minimum CFEI requirements had been withdrawn during 2025 and that existing federal requirements remained in place [3]. Because rulemakings and local code adoption can change, purchasers should check the current DOE text and the applicable local energy and mechanical codes before putting a specific compliance claim in a purchase order.

That is a reason to verify the rule, not a reason to avoid efficiency data. CFEI remains a useful way to ask better questions of competing large-diameter ceiling fans.

## The bottom line

**Use CFEI to evaluate rated energy performance, but never let it stand in for an airflow layout, comfort assessment or installation review.** Request the exact model's rating basis; compare like with like; and judge the finished job by measured results against the building's stated objective.
